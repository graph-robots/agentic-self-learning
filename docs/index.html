Robot RSI?

A Controlled Study of Models, Skills, Feedback, and Curriculum in Agentic Robotics

Kaiyuan Chen1, Xinyue Zhu1, Shuangyu Xie1, Uksang Yoo1, Thi Thu Phuong Luong2, Marcus Gualtieri2, Christian Juette2, Liu Ren2, Spencer Huang3, Ken Goldberg1

1University of California, Berkeley2Bosch3NVIDIAarXiv:2611.09030

Paper Code Agent traces VABAR benchmark

In robot recursive self-improvement (RSI), a coding agent writes a robot policy as a program, runs it in simulation, reads what happened, and rewrites it.

Self-learning is limited less by the availability of useful information than by the agent's ability to turn the information into effective policy updates.

We hold the loop fixed and vary one part at a time (the model, the skills, the feedback and the curriculum) across 9 manipulation tasks, 631 experiment configurations and 28K coding-agent rollouts.

Part 1 · The problem

Robot failures don't explain themselves

In software, a failing test points at a line of code. In a robot, where you see a failure () is often far from where it was decided (). Three kinds of failure come up again and again.

a step of the programthe robot stood still✕ where you see it · ◆ where it was decidedtraced back✓ the step reported ok

Motion planning · Syringe Packing

▸ Scrub the ribbon, or ask where the drop was decided.

What you see
Every step reports ok. Each syringe is still flat when it is let go, and lands across the lid.
Where it was decided
At the grasp. From then on the 150 mm syringe is part of the moving robot, and carried flat it cannot pass the 55 mm opening. The motion has to account for the held object, obstacles and clearance together.

What you see
Pressed against the first peg, the cable springs back off when the arm lets go, woven round neither peg.

    Finding 1 · Model

    Models look alike when they write the first policy. They separate when they repair it.

    Overall held-out success over two rounds of the same execution feedback, and what each model's edits did.

    ▸ Step through the two rounds of feedback.

    Opus 5, Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra and Sonnet 5 all start at 43–45% overall success. After two rounds of the same execution feedback, Opus 5 reaches 74% and Fable 5.1 64%, while GPT-6 Astra and Sonnet 5 stay at 48% and 46%. Opus 5.5 goes from 67 to 77. Writing a plausible policy and repairing one from evidence are distinct capabilities.

    The squares show where the difference comes from. Each square is one edit, classified by its outcome: fixed and success rose, fixed with no gain, the same failure, or a different failure. Some of Opus 5's and Fable 5.1's edits fixed the failure and raised success; none of Sonnet 5's or GPT-6 Astra's did.

    Finding 2 · Skills

    Expert skills are a boundary for weaker models and a scaffold for stronger ones.

    Each model's first program, by where every line came from.

    ▸ Take away everything the library provided.

    How to read a field

    Dashed fence: the skill library the agent was given

    Inside, from the centre out

    • expert task skills
    • general skills the human also used
    • general skills the human did not use

    Outside

    • new code the model wrote itself

    1 dot ≈ 20 lines

    @0 → @2 overall success: the first program, then after two rounds of feedback

    After Human, models run from the lowest @2 to the highest.

    dashed fence = the library · 1 dot ≈ 20 lines

    ends lower after two roundsends higher
    Lines per first program, averaged over each model's runs per task and then over the tasks.

    We traced every line of each first program to its source: the expert task skills, the general skills the human policy also uses, the remaining general skills, or none of them, which makes it new code. New code makes up 24% of the lines for Opus 5, 18% for Opus 5.5 and 12% for Fable 5.1; Sonnet 5, DeepSeek V4, Kimi K3 and GPT-6 Astra write only 4–7%.

    The models that improve most with feedback are the ones that step outside the fence: they use the expert skills as a basis and add or change task-specific logic where the provided code is not enough. The others compose behaviour the library already has. Several of them start from similar success, so the difference is not the first program but whether the agent can modify and extend the skills it was given.

    Kimi K3

    Using more of the library is not the same as adapting beyond it: Kimi K3 uses the largest share of general skills not used by the human policy (13% of its lines), yet gains only 7 points.

      World coordinates contain everything needed to work out a grasp offset or a relative orientation, yet agents often fail to compose those transforms. Writing the same state in object frames helps, and naming the parts that matter, the wrench's ring and the hook's shank, helps more: 52% overall success with part annotations, against 40% for object frames, 30% for world-frame state and 19% for the outcome alone. On Tool Hanging: 15, 25, 63 and 90%.

        In one run, the privileged policy pinched a narrow tab on the pot handle, which is only a few millimeters wide, and reached 90–100% held-out success; after the switch to perception it dropped to 0–30%.
        
          
        The agent kept naming the tab grasp as the cause of the failures, yet changed its reach and retries rather than the grasp.
        
        
        
        
        
          
        Gear Meshing · VABAR
        in one run
        
          
        A camera that barely sees the pegs
        
          
        
            
            
          
        
          
          
        
          
        The inherited policy read the pegs from the front camera, where each occupies only 2–3 raised depth pixels, instead of the overhead camera (about 18). It fell to at most 7%; a perception-only run using the overhead camera reached 97–100%.
        
          
        Later iterations mainly tried to repair peg detection rather than change the inherited camera.
        
        
        
      
        
    
        

    
        
      
        
        
        The privileged phase learns useful structure in Threading (86% vs 2% without it), Coffee and Mug Cleanup, but it can also commit early to choices perception cannot support: Tool Hanging falls to 16% vs 63%; overall 52 vs 46. After the switch, agents patch the inherited program rather than reorganize it: 12.0 → 12.7 skills on average, while perception-only programs end at 16.1.
        
        
        Exact geometry is not the same as knowing what to do with it. In Tool Hanging the privileged phase stays below 10% held-out success even with the exact wrench pose: the pose says where the wrench is, not where or how to hold it for the carry.

        Part 3 · Coverage
        
      
        How much of the problem do today's agents cover?
        
      
        Take the 73 VABAR Level-1 test cases that a human-written policy solves. Zero-shot generation alone solves only 7.3%. Iterative self-learning covers 16.1%, richer simulation feedback 10.5%, human task skills 27.0% and a newer model 35.0%; 4.1% remain unsolved. What the agents were missing is mostly spatial reasoning (49.9%), then motion planning (14.4%), skill composition (11.6%), manipulation planning (8.8%) and perception (8.0%).
